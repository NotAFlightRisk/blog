<TryIt label="Amount" />

In Chrome with its menus in English, the comma never goes in. You're left with 504, and nothing anywhere says anything went wrong. Firefox tells you it's not a number, which is at least honest.

## Chrome bins the comma as you type

Blink [checks each keystroke](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/151.0.7922.34/third_party/blink/renderer/core/html/forms/number_input_type.cc#L244-L247) against `0123456789.Ee-+` plus the locale's own number characters, and the code that [builds that list](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/151.0.7922.34/third_party/blink/renderer/platform/text/platform_locale.cc#L280-L290) leaves one out on purpose:

```cpp title="platform_locale.cc"
// We don't accept group separators.
if (i != kGroupSeparatorIndex)
  builder.Append(decimal_symbols_[i]);
```

In English the comma is the group separator, so it's gone before your page ever sees it. `input.value` is "504" and `validity.badInput` is false, so there's nothing to catch.

The locale is Chrome's own menu language, not your page's. An `<input lang="de-DE">` in an English Chromium still gave me 504, and starting Chromium in German gave 5.04. Firefox goes by the page's `lang` instead, so it's bad input on an English page and 5.04 on a German one.

## The libraries do it too

Last week someone in Costa Rica [posted on Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50121562) about being charged $504 for $5.04 of API credit. Another commenter, in Germany with their Linux set to English, said they've ended up selling things on Amazon and eBay "for 599,00 instead of 5.99".

How many of the libraries people put on money fields would do the same? I took the 20 most downloaded npm packages that turn a typed amount into a number (parsers, masks and input components), set no locale or separator options, and ran them in an English browser on an English page. Inputs and masks got typed into a key at a time (all but ngx-mask in Chromium and Firefox), and the plain parsers I just called.

13 of the 20 make 504 of it, and they're not obscure ones. [@internationalized/number](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@internationalized/number) is Adobe's, and it's what React Aria's NumberField parses with. rc-input-number and its renamed successor are what Ant Design's InputNumber is built on.

The German amount is worse. 18 of the 20 turn a thousand-odd euros into one and a bit, and I've no idea where inputmask's 1.56 comes from. Only globalize and parse-decimal-number say no to both.

## Why 504

In US English a comma groups thousands, so the quick way to read "$1,234" is to delete the commas, and that's what most of these do. rc-input-number's whole default parser is [one line](https://github.com/react-component/input-number/blob/v9.5.0/src/InputNumber.tsx#L217-L218), with a comment on what it's for:

```ts title="src/InputNumber.tsx"
// [Legacy] We still support auto convert `$ 123,456` to `123456`
return parsedStr.replace(/[^\w.-]+/g, '');
```

But nobody in the US writes 5,04. Groups come in threes, so a comma with two digits after it can't be grouping anything. It's someone typing a decimal the other way round. globalize and parse-decimal-number check for that, and nothing else does:

```text title="node"
> currency('5,04').value
504
> numeral('5,04').value()
504
> new NumberParser('en-US').parse('5,04')
504
> parseDecimalNumber('5,04')
NaN
```

## A German browser just flips it

Two of the 13 only made 504 because my browser was in English. react-currency-input-field and React Aria's NumberField (the field on top of @internationalized/number) take their separators from the browser's language when you don't give them any. In a German browser they read 5,04 fine, and turn 5.04 into 504 instead. The Costa Rica poster says that's their case, with settings that use a comma and 5.04 typed in (the company's reply in the thread says 5,04, so I'm not sure which).

Every library's locale option works, for what it's worth. Give react-number-format or rc-input-number `decimalSeparator=","`, or react-currency-input-field a German `intlConfig`, and a typed 5,04 comes out as 5.04. That just moves the problem to whoever types a dot.

## Catching it

First you need what they actually typed, so it's a text input rather than `type="number"`, since Chrome's binned the comma by the time you look. [`inputmode="decimal"`](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Global_attributes/inputmode) gets phones a number pad. Then check it:

```js
// commas only ever group threes
const looksWrong = (typed) => /,(?!\d{3}(\D|$))/.test(typed);
```

That catches 5,04, 599,00 and 1.234,56 and lets 1,234.56 through. Swap the comma for a dot on a German page. When it fires, ask what they meant rather than charging them for it.

It won't catch 5,040, which comes out as five thousand and forty here and in globalize and parse-decimal-number too. Nothing can tell that one from the string.
