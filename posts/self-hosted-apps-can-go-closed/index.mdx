Pick something you self-host. Could the people behind it put the next release out under terms that aren't open source, without asking anyone?

I went through the 50 most-starred apps on [awesome-selfhosted](https://awesome-selfhosted.net), its non-free list included, to find out. For 37 of them the answer is yes, or they already have. The AGPL badge on the repo doesn't tell you which group yours is in.

| Licence | Apps |
| --- | --- |
| Already ship some non-open code (11) | Dify, Open WebUI, Stirling-PDF, LobeHub, NocoDB, Meilisearch, Twenty, Metabase, Rocket.Chat, Outline, Novu |
| Permissive (18) | Ollama, Hoppscotch, code-server, AnythingLLM, Caddy, Traefik, Memos, PocketBase, Gitea, Appwrite, LocalAI, LibreChat, Hyperswitch, Reactive Resume, Kong, Appsmith, MindsDB, Umami |
| Copyleft with a company CLA (8) | AppFlowy, Plane, Odoo, Discourse, Puter, ToolJet, PhotoPrism, Halo |
| Copyleft, no CLA (11) | Immich, Syncthing, Vaultwarden, Pi-hole, Penpot, Jellyfin, SiYuan, paperless-ngx, RSSHub, Sunshine, ERPNext |
| Permissive, owned by a foundation (2) | Home Assistant, Apache Airflow |

The first three rows are the 37. That's counting what's in each public repo as of 17 October, read by hand, and plenty of apps in the other rows sell a closed enterprise edition from somewhere else already. Odoo and Kong for a start. I ran the next 50 as well and it drops to 31, as further down there are more volunteer projects like SearXNG and Invidious with no company to do it. That's 68 of the top 100.

## NocoDB did it in one commit

NocoDB is a self-hosted Airtable with 65,000 stars, and untill this year it was AGPL. On 8 January a commit called [`chore: change to sustainable use license`](https://github.com/nocodb/nocodb/commit/d98ad39c9b147be0fbd4e1e6ffbf354283b0b4e1) deleted the 661-line AGPL and put 61 lines of the Sustainable Use License in it's place, the same licence n8n uses. The last AGPL release, 0.265.1, came out in October. Everything since 13 January says this instead:

> You may use or modify the software only for your own internal business purposes or for non-commercial or personal use.

GitHub lists 336 contributors to that repo. None of them had to be asked, and a week or so later a user [said as much](https://github.com/nocodb/nocodb/discussions/12891):

> For the record, this project uses a CLA, so there isn't a legal issue with changing the license of external contributions.

A CLA, or contributor licence agreement, is something you sign before your PR gets merged, and NocoDB's contributing guide has asked for one since 2022. The founder's reason for the switch, [posted on Cloudron's forum](https://forum.cloudron.io/topic/14918/heads-up-nocodb-is-no-longer-open-source.), was companies running private forks and ignoring the AGPL, with lawyers too pricey to chase them. I've a bit of sympathy for that. But it was NocoDB's call alone, and the [licence page](https://nocodb.com/docs/self-hosting/license) says "The license protects contributors", which is one way of putting it.

## The CLA's the bit that matters

A copyleft licence is meant to stop exactly this. If I send a fix to an AGPL project, my fix is AGPL, and nobody can ship it closed without my say-so. A CLA is where I give that say-so up front.

AppFlowy, Plane, Puter, ToolJet and PhotoPrism are all AGPL today, and all have a CLA. So do Odoo, Discourse and Halo on the other GPLs. I read all eight agreements and every one lets the company license your code on other terms. PhotoPrism's [is the bluntest](https://web.photoprism.app/cla/):

> this includes the right to apply any license to the Contributions that PhotoPrism considers fit for the respective purpose as decided by PhotoPrism in its sole discretion, including other open-source, copyleft, and proprietary licenses

None of that is hidden, and people have been asking about it for ages. Discourse's own forum has a [thread from 2013](https://meta.discourse.org/t/1399) titled "Does Discourse's contributor agreement provide an exit door from Open Source?". Discourse is still GPL 13 years later, so having the door doesn't mean they'll use it.

Not every CLA is a door, either. Postiz, 61st by stars, uses the FSFE's [Fiduciary License Agreement](https://github.com/gitroomhq/postiz-app/blob/main/ICLA.md), which only lets them license your code under terms that are both free software and open source. So it's worth reading what a CLA actually says.

Companies do put the door in early though. Every copyleft app in the top 50 that moved with a CLA had it in place long before. Metabase's was more than four years ahead of its `enterprise/` folder, and NocoDB's nearly four years ahead of January.

It's on my own box too. My analytics run on [Plausible](https://github.com/plausible/analytics), which is AGPL with a CLA bot on every PR, and has an `extra/` folder whose licence grants "no rights to use, distribute or otherwise exploit this software". The community edition I run doesn't compile it in. My error tracker, [Bugsink](https://github.com/bugsink/bugsink), was never open source to begin with, and I picked it anyway.

## MIT never needed one

The permissive row is the biggest, and it's the one people worry abut least. MIT and Apache let anyone take the code and ship a closed version, as long as they keep the licence and notices, and that includes the people who wrote it. Contributors' code comes in under the same terms, so there's nothing to sign.

That's how most of the first row got there, and the newest one isn't in the top 50 at all. ThingsBoard, 100th by stars, swapped Apache 2.0 for the Business Source License in [4.4](https://github.com/thingsboard/thingsboard/releases/tag/v4.4), out on 29 September. Free use is now capped at 1,000 devices, or 100 on one server if it's for business, and needs a free licence key from them. The release notes don't mention it, and awesome-selfhosted still lists it as Apache.

The first row's a mix, mind. NocoDB, Outline, Dify, LobeHub and Open WebUI put the whole app under their own terms. Metabase, Rocket.Chat, Twenty, Meilisearch, Novu and Stirling-PDF keep most of it open and fence off the paid bits in a folder or in marked files.

Home Assistant and Airflow are permissive too. Anyone could make a closed fork of either, but the official ones belong to the Open Home Foundation and the Apache Software Foundation, which exist to keep them open.

## Stirling-PDF didn't need a CLA either

Copyleft with no CLA should be the safe row. Contributors licensed their code as GPL or AGPL and nothing else, so the owners can't take it closed without every one of them agreeing. A DCO doesn't change that, it's only a sign-off saying you've the right to submit the code, and it hands over nothing else.

Stirling-PDF was in that row until September 2024, when [a single commit](https://github.com/Stirling-Tools/Stirling-PDF/commit/da6b66e1) swapped its GPL-3.0 for MIT, taking out 674 lines and putting in 21. An `app/proprietary/` folder came the next year. I couldn't find a PR or anything asking past contributors, which doesn't mean nobody did.

The rest of the row isn't quite as locked as it looks either. Immich went from MIT to AGPL in February 2024 and RSSHub only last December, so anyone can still take their MIT-era code closed. Sunshine's owner had contributors assign it their copyright from 2022 untill it dropped its CLA in 2024. And Syncthing and Penpot are MPL 2.0, which works file by file, so a brand new file can be proprietary.

## What changes for you

Whatever's allready been released stays under the licence it shipped with. NocoDB's 0.265.1 is AGPL forever and anyone can fork it, though I couldn't find anyone keeping up an AGPL fork.

If you just run these at home, the licences that came in still let you. What changes is where the new stuff goes. Twenty's SSO arrived in 2024 as commercial-only files, and an [issue from August](https://github.com/nocodb/nocodb/issues/14357) points out NocoDB's new documents API needs an Enterprise licence on self-hosted.

To check an app that isn't on the list, look for "CLA" in its contributing guide, then at the checks on a few merged PRs:

```sh
$ gh pr list -R makeplane/plane --state merged -L 20 \
    --json statusCheckRollup \
    --jq '[.[].statusCheckRollup[] | .context // .name]
          | unique | map(select(test("cla"; "i")))'
["license/cla"]
```

That won't catch a CLA that's only a file in the repo, like Open WebUI's, and either way it's the agreement itself you want to read.
